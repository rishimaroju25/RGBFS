# Limitations

**Author:** Salah

Our results should be read with the following limitations in mind. The first five affect the main conclusions; the rest affect how far the methods can be trusted or reused.

**1. Few stress episodes.** Our rule finds 13 stress episodes from 2005 to 2026, and only 9 fall in the 2010-2026 period where the models are tested out of sample. Three of those are overlapping legs of the 2022 bear market. With so few events, a single episode can move a headline number by 10 percentage points or more, and none of our comparisons is statistically conclusive. Our results describe these 13 episodes; they are not reliable estimates of how the signals would perform on the next crisis.

**2. What counts as a warning changes the answer.** The team ran two Week 6 tests that define a warning differently (Figure 5). In the lead-time test, a signal counts as warning of an episode if it was on at any point in the 60 trading days before the start; under that rule the network anomaly signal warned of 8 of 13 episodes with 0.57 false alarms a year. In the false-alarm test, the alert has to go off within 20 trading days before the start, and an alert that stays on for months counts as a false alarm; under that rule the best network signal warned of 1 of 9 episodes. Neither definition is wrong, but they answer different questions, and the report's main claims depend on which one is used.

**3. The results depend on the period.** Under the stricter test, every early warning came in 2022-2026, and the early-warning models beat a random guess only in that period (PR-AUC 0.22 against a base rate of 0.08). No signal warned of the 2011, 2015 or 2018 episodes or of COVID-19 under that test. Sudden shocks with no build-up (the 2010 flash crash, February 2018, COVID-19) are the hardest case, and no signal anticipated them reliably.

**4. How stress is defined.** A stress day is one where the S&P 500's 21-day realized volatility, centered on that day, is at least 25%. The threshold was chosen after looking at the full history: 20% gives 17 episodes and 30% gives 8. Because the labels are built from volatility, and the VIX is the market's own estimate of volatility, detecting stress on the same day is close to automatic (Figure 3), so we judge the system only on warnings made in advance. The centered window also means each label uses 10 trading days of future returns; we used the labels only as targets, never as model inputs (`docs/lookahead_checklist.md`).

**5. Survivorship and missing data.** Yahoo Finance drops a company's price history after it is acquired or delisted. About 23% of S&P 500 member-days have no price (62% coverage in 2008, 92% in 2022), and the final return of failed firms is never recorded. Our stock-level measures are therefore computed mostly on firms that survived, which likely understates stress in 2008, when the missing firms were concentrated among those that failed. Some gaps are simply renamed tickers, which further mapping could recover (`docs/survivorship_bias.md`).

**6. Text data.** Company filings arrive only once a quarter, so a sentiment signal from them changes slowly and cannot react to a crisis that builds over a few weeks. In our manual check, FinBERT agreed with a human reader on 12 of 19 filing paragraphs (63%); most disagreements were standard risk-factor language scored as negative, and the model also missed real bad news written in hedged "may" language. We reduced the first problem by scoring management discussion separately and keeping only new or changed risk-factor text, but the model's reading of filings is still imperfect. *[Fatima: confirm which corpus the final sentiment results use, 5 firms in 2022 or 50 firms from 2006, and edit this sentence to match.]*

**7. Modeling choices.** The network results depend on choices we fixed early: removing the market-wide move before measuring correlations, keeping the strongest 5% of links, and using 60-day windows. A 60-day window both delays a signal and keeps it high for up to three months after a shock. An earlier version of the network (all pairs with correlation above 0.55) mostly tracked market-wide correlation, and the 60-day absorption ratio had a bug, fixed in Week 7, that held it near 1.0. Sector labels use each company's current sector for all dates. Our models are deliberately simple (linear and logistic regression), and alert thresholds were fixed in advance rather than tuned, so better-tuned models could do better or worse.

**8. Data timing.** The St. Louis Fed and Chicago Fed stress indexes are published several days after the week they describe; we delayed them accordingly, but the two parts of our code use slightly different delays (4 and 5 trading days). FRED serves the latest revised history, not the values first published, and the Chicago Fed index is re-estimated every week, so its historical values contain some information that was not available at the time. Our return cleaning also uses one or two days of future prices to spot data errors; it removes 0.215% of returns.

**9. Scope.** We study one market (large U.S. stocks), at a daily frequency, from 2005. We do not test any decision rule built on the alerts, such as reducing risk when an alert fires, so we cannot say whether a warning would have been worth acting on after costs.

**10. Use in practice.** Even the best signals produced between 0.5 and 2 false alarms a year. A public warning system with that record could prompt unnecessary selling, and a widely followed warning could become self-fulfilling. Our system is a research tool and should not be used to make investment or policy decisions.
